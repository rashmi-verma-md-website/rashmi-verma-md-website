Conditions we treat

Optic Neuritis

Optic neuritis is inflammation of the optic nerve, the cable that carries what the eye sees to the brain. It usually affects one eye and often improves over weeks to months, but finding the cause matters for protecting your vision and your long-term health.

When to get emergency care

Sudden vision loss needs to be checked the same day. If vision loss comes with weakness, numbness, trouble speaking or a severe headache, call 911.

Common symptoms

  • Blurred, dim or lost vision in one eye that develops over hours to a few days
  • Pain behind the eye, often worse when you move your eye
  • Colors, especially red, looking faded or washed out
  • A dark or blurry spot in the center of your vision

In children, optic neuritis more often affects both eyes and may follow a viral illness.

What causes it?

Optic neuritis happens when the immune system attacks the coating (myelin) around the optic nerve. It can be:

  • Linked to multiple sclerosis (MS). Optic neuritis is sometimes the first sign of MS.
  • Part of another demyelinating disease, such as neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) or MOG antibody–associated disease (MOGAD). These need different treatment, so telling them apart is important.
  • Related to infection or other inflammatory conditions, such as sarcoidosis.

How it is diagnosed

Dr. Verma checks your vision, color vision, pupils and the optic nerve, and may order:

  • Visual field testing to map any blind spots
  • OCT imaging to measure swelling or thinning of the optic nerve
  • MRI of the brain and eye sockets to look for inflammation and signs of MS
  • Blood tests for antibodies linked to NMOSD and MOGAD, when appropriate

Treatment and recovery

Many people recover much of their vision over several weeks to months. High-dose steroids may be recommended to speed recovery, and prompt treatment is especially important when NMOSD or MOGAD is suspected. Some people notice that colors or contrast stay slightly different after recovery.

If tests suggest MS or another demyelinating disease, Dr. Verma works with your neurologist so you can start long-term treatment that lowers the risk of future attacks.

How Dr. Verma can help

Dr. Verma is trained in both neurology and neuro-ophthalmology. She looks at the eye, the optic nerve and the brain together, explains your results in plain language, and works closely with your eye doctor, neurologist and other specialists.
