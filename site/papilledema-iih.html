Conditions we treat

Papilledema and Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension (IIH)

Papilledema is swelling of the optic nerves caused by raised pressure inside the skull. Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) is one of its most common causes. Without treatment, the pressure can slowly and permanently damage vision, often without warning.

When to get emergency care

If your vision is getting worse quickly, or you have a sudden severe headache, go to the nearest emergency room or call 911.

Common symptoms

  • Headaches, often daily and sometimes worse when lying down or in the morning
  • Brief episodes of dimmed or lost vision lasting seconds, often when standing up or bending over
  • A whooshing sound in the ears that pulses with the heartbeat
  • Double vision
  • Blurred vision or loss of side vision

Some people have no symptoms, and the swelling is found during a routine eye exam.

What causes it?

Papilledema can be caused by brain tumors, blood clots in the brain's veins, infections, bleeding or other conditions, so it always needs prompt brain imaging.

When no other cause is found, the diagnosis is idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH). IIH is most common in young women, especially after recent weight gain, but it can also occur in older women, men and children. Some medicines, such as tetracycline antibiotics and high doses of vitamin A, can contribute.

How it is diagnosed

  • Dilated eye exam to see and grade the optic nerve swelling
  • OCT imaging to measure the swelling precisely
  • Visual field testing to check for loss of side vision
  • MRI and MR venography of the brain to look for other causes
  • Lumbar puncture (spinal tap) to measure the pressure of the fluid around the brain and test the fluid

Treatment

  • Medication such as acetazolamide, which reduces the fluid that builds up pressure
  • Weight management, which can lower the pressure for many patients. Dr. Verma coordinates with weight management programs.
  • Headache treatment, in coordination with your neurologist
  • Surgery when vision is at risk, such as optic nerve sheath fenestration, a shunt, or venous sinus stenting, performed by surgical colleagues

Because vision can worsen without obvious symptoms, regular visits with visual field testing and OCT are an important part of care.

How Dr. Verma can help

Dr. Verma is trained in both neurology and neuro-ophthalmology. She looks at the eye, the optic nerve and the brain together, explains your results in plain language, and works closely with your eye doctor, neurologist and other specialists.
